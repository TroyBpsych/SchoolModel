The Three Phases

How Students Progress
Through In-Person School

Students advance when they demonstrate readiness — not when the calendar says so. Each phase has a distinct learning environment, daily rhythm, and set of goals. Toggle between phases to see a sample day for each.

Roots Phase · Ages 5–10

The Foundation of Self, Relationship & Literacy

Play is the primary learning mode. No worksheets, no standardized tests. Guided play stations for literacy and numeracy, rich outdoor time, pretend play, music, and making. One guide stays with each child for 2–3 years in a mixed-age group of 12.

The goal is not early academics — it is the development of executive function, emotional regulation, language, and the love of learning that makes all future academics possible.

Day: 8:30am–2:30pm · 6 hours · 85+ min outdoors · No DI segment >15 min

Workshop Phase · Ages 10–14

Learning Through Making, Building & Real Problems

Eight-week interdisciplinary project cycles are the spine. Daily Foundations Block for direct instruction in literacy and numeracy. Elective tracks let students begin developing their distinctive strengths. A Faculty Advisor meets with each student weekly.

This phase specifically counters the documented creativity slump at ages 9–12 — the window where standardized schooling most aggressively suppresses divergent thinking. The design protects it.

Day: 8:30am–3:30pm · 7 hours · 60+ min outdoors · No class before 8:30am

Field Phase · Ages 14–18

Real Contribution, Real Mentors & Real Stakes

Two days per week in community internships with business owners, entrepreneurs, artists, and practitioners. School is where students debrief, go deeper, and connect experience to knowledge. The Senior Contribution project — a year-long student-defined real-world project — culminates this phase.

No letter grades. Competency transcripts. AI maintains a live mastery map reviewed weekly with the advisor. Students advance when they're ready.

Day: 8:30am–3:30pm · Internship 2 days/week · Flexible start 8:30–9:00am

Family Partnership — In-Person School

The APA's review of 448 studies is clear: what families do matters as much as what the school does. In the In-Person model, families are not passive drop-off parents — they are research-defined partners. The school handles the full instructional program. The family activates everything research shows happens best at home. Together they produce outcomes neither can achieve alone.

APA 448-study review · Harvard Family Research Project · National PTA Six Standards · CASA 10-year study · Snow & Beals 2006

On Campus — How Families Are Involved at School
Every 8 Weeks

Public Exhibition Night

Students present completed project work to family, community members, and outside experts. Families are not an audience — they are co-assessors who ask real questions and give real feedback. Research shows public accountability to people who matter drives deeper learning and higher student motivation than any test.

Twice Yearly

Student-Led Portfolio Conference

The student leads the conference — walking family and advisor through their portfolio of work. Not a teacher-led report card meeting. Research shows student-led conferences build self-advocacy, metacognition, and ownership of learning. The family sees the full picture of who their child is becoming, not just a grade.

Every 4–6 Weeks

Proactive Advisor Contact

Every family receives a proactive call, note, or brief from their child's advisor — not reserved for problems. The APA review identifies two-way communication as the active ingredient in family engagement. Advisors celebrate growth, invite family input, and ensure families always feel informed and included.

Quarterly

Family Advisory Council

Families hold real governance roles — not just feedback surveys. The council provides genuine input on curriculum themes, school policies, and community partnerships. National PTA research identifies decision-making partnership as one of the six standards of effective family engagement. Decisions and responses are published transparently.

Throughout the Year

Families as Project Contributors

When a family member's professional expertise, life experience, or cultural knowledge connects to the current project cycle, they are invited to contribute — as guest practitioners, storytellers, or project consultants. This activates the research finding that students whose families are visible contributors to school life show higher engagement and stronger identity as learners.

Open Studio Policy

Families May Observe Any Time

Families are welcome to observe Project Studio time, elective tracks, and community events with advance notice. Seeing learning in action — rather than just receiving reports about it — builds the understanding and trust that the family partnership research shows is essential for sustained engagement.

At Home — What Research Shows Families Should Do After School
Every Night

Family Dinner Together

The CASA 10-year longitudinal study found teens eating 7 family dinners per week were 40% more likely to receive A's and B's. Harvard research shows dinner table conversation outperforms read-aloud as a vocabulary builder — children absorb sophisticated "rare words" naturally from adult stories. The school provides a Dinner Table Conversation Guide each week: 3 questions tied to the current project cycle. No screens at the table. No homework to rush through. Just conversation.

Weekly

Family Learning Guide Activities

Each project cycle comes with a Family Learning Guide — 3–5 conversation starters, one book recommendation, and one optional activity tied to what the child is working on at school. These are not homework assignments. They are high-leverage at-home engagement tools grounded in the APA finding that at-home discussion and encouragement are among the strongest academic supports a family can provide. Takes 20–30 minutes. Builds knowledge, language, and the sense that learning happens everywhere — not only at school.

Daily for Young Children

Reading Together (Roots Phase)

For children in the Roots Phase (ages 5–10), reading together at home is one of the highest-return activities available. The Harvard Home-School Study found that families who read with their children and take them to the library regularly produce measurably stronger language outcomes through 6th grade. The school provides monthly book lists matched to each child's phase and project cycle. No pressure — just reading because it matters and because it's enjoyable.

Always

Express Belief — Specifically

The APA review of 448 studies found that family encouragement and expressed belief in the child is one of the most consistent predictors of academic achievement across all ages. This doesn't mean generic praise. Research on growth mindset shows that specific, process-focused encouragement is most powerful: "I noticed how you kept working on that even when it got hard" rather than "You're so smart." The school's monthly newsletter includes specific language families can use.

Non-Negotiable

Protect Sleep

The research on sleep and academic performance is unambiguous — and protecting it is entirely a family-side responsibility. AAP recommendations: ages 6–12 need 9–12 hours; ages 13–18 need 8–10 hours. No screens after 9pm. Consistent sleep and wake times even on weekends. The school schedules no homework that competes with sleep, starts no earlier than 8:30am, and communicates the sleep research directly to families at orientation and annually. Families protect the hours the school cannot.

Weekend & After School

Movement, Outdoor Play & Free Time

The school delivers 90+ minutes of movement daily — but research suggests children benefit from movement across the full day and week, not just during school hours. Outdoor play after school, weekend bike rides, sports leagues, and unstructured nature time all compound the school's movement program. Improvised outdoor play specifically (child-designed games, free exploration) develops the divergent thinking that sits at the heart of creativity. The school provides a Home Movement Guide with simple weekly suggestions.

What the Research Says NOT to Do at Home
Avoid

Helping With Homework

The APA's 448-study review found parental homework help has a small negative effect on academic achievement — especially in secondary school. It removes the productive struggle that builds competence and can create conflict and dependency. The Groundwork Model assigns no traditional homework precisely because of this evidence. If your child asks for help, ask questions rather than give answers.

Avoid

Hovering and Over-Managing

Self-Determination Theory is unambiguous: children develop stronger intrinsic motivation, deeper learning, and greater self-regulation when they experience genuine autonomy. "Helicopter parenting" — solving problems before children encounter them, managing their social relationships, advocating too aggressively with teachers — undermines the very independence the school is building. Trust the process. Be available, not ever-present.

Avoid

Screens Competing with Connection

The research on family dinner, evening conversation, and reading together all share one prerequisite: the family is actually present and engaged together. Screens — phones at dinner, TV during family time, devices in bedrooms after 9pm — directly displace the activities research shows matter most. The school communicates the screen research directly and provides a simple family screen protocol. The most impactful thing most families can do is phones away at dinner. Every night.

The Research in One Sentence

All families can effectively support their children's learning when given the right guidance — the gap between highly involved and less involved families is largely a gap in information and school support, not in motivation or love. The Groundwork Model is designed to close that gap.

How Each Phase Works
Across Campus & Home Days

Each phase adapts the hybrid rhythm differently. The campus days always anchor community, movement, and instruction. Home days deepen the project work and activate the family partnership research. Toggle between phases to see a sample campus day for each.

Roots Phase · Ages 5–10

Play on Campus, Play at Home

Campus days are rich play environments: outdoor movement, guided play stations, project and dramatic play, music, and arts. Home days mirror this — the Family Play Guide gives parents specific play activities, books, outdoor explorations, and songs that extend what's happening at school.

The Roots Phase is especially well-suited to hybrid because young children naturally learn through family life — reading together, outdoor play, and mealtime conversation are all home-native activities that the research shows are high-leverage.

Workshop Phase · Ages 10–14

Project Launched on Campus, Continued at Home

Campus days deliver the elements that need physical co-presence: direct instruction, teacher-coached project work, peer collaboration, movement, and advisory. Home days Tue/Thu continue the 8-week project using the Home Day Project Guide — researching, making, writing, or building independently.

The Thursday afternoon advisor video check-in maintains relationship continuity and keeps each student on track between campus days.

Campus: M/W 8:30–3:30 · Fri 8:30–12:30 · Home: Project Guide + AI tools · Thu advisor video

Field Phase · Ages 14–18

Community, Campus, and Internship in Balance

In the Field Phase, hybrid works naturally with the internship schedule. One or both home days can be internship days — meaning the student spends Tuesday or Thursday at their community placement rather than at home. Campus days anchor the seminar, debrief, and Senior Contribution work.

Friday's half-day is the key community touchpoint — students from across the Field Phase gather for exhibition prep, guest practitioners, and peer connection.

Campus: M/W 8:30–3:30 · Fri 8:30–12:30 · Internship: 1–2 home days/week · Advisor weekly

Tuesday & Thursday —
Structured, Not Free-Form

Project Continuation + Foundations Practice

Reading + Family Learning + Free Time

  • 12:00–1:00: Communal lunch at home. Use the Dinner Table Conversation Guide. Then free outdoor play — unstructured, child-directed. Nature preferred.
  • 15-min video check-in (Thursday afternoons): Student + advisor. Review home day progress, preview next campus day, address any questions.

The Three Phases in Independent Study

How Each Phase Works
in the Home Setting

Each phase adapts differently for the home environment. The Roots Phase is naturally home-suited — play, reading, and outdoor exploration are home-native. The Workshop Phase requires the most active facilitation. The Field Phase is where independent study is often at its most powerful. Toggle between phases to see a sample home day for each.

Roots Phase · Ages 5–10

Play is the Curriculum — Home is Ideal

The home environment is naturally the richest play environment available. Reading together, outdoor exploration, music, imaginative play, and hands-on making are all home-native activities the research shows are highest-leverage for ages 5–10. No AI tools before age 8. No worksheets ever.

The parent-child relationship in this phase can be the closest thing to the ideal Roots environment — when structured with the Groundwork guides and held accountable through weekly advisor sessions and monthly community days.

Day: 8:30am–2:30pm · Play guide · Outdoor 90+ min · Rest period · No AI before age 8

Workshop Phase · Ages 10–14

Projects + Community + Active Facilitation

This phase requires the most active family facilitation. Eight-week project cycles need real materials, dedicated space, and an engaged adult. AI adaptive tools handle the personalized foundations practice. The monthly Community Days are most important here — peer collaboration during the creativity slump window (ages 9–12) is non-negotiable.

Local co-ops, community sports leagues, and neighborhood groups supplement the peer connection that monthly campus days provide. Weekly advisor video sessions track mastery and adjust the learning plan.

Day: 9:00am–2:30pm · AI adaptive tools · Project Guide · Weekly advisor · Monthly community

Field Phase · Ages 14–18

The Most Powerful Home Phase

The Field Phase is where independent study is at its most powerful. Family professional networks source internships. Entrepreneurially-minded parents model real-world contribution directly. The 20% entrepreneur mentor effect is most accessible for families already embedded in professional or business communities.

The Senior Contribution project — the year-long student-defined real-world project — is fully supported at home. Advisor weekly sessions review the mastery map and guide the project trajectory.

What Independent Study Requires — and What It Cannot Fully Replace

The research is honest: some elements of the Groundwork model are harder to fully replicate at home. Families choosing Independent Study should understand these limitations and how the model addresses them.

Limitation

Daily Peer Interaction

Children need peers. Research on social development is clear. Monthly community days, community sports leagues, co-ops, and neighborhood play groups are essential supplements — not optional.

Limitation

Diverse Adult Relationships

The advisor provides one key adult relationship. The community mentor network provides others. Families must actively pursue varied adult mentorship — community organizations, sports coaches, faith communities, clubs.

Limitation

Parent Capacity

Independent Study requires a dedicated adult at home who has completed Groundwork training and can maintain the structure. It is not appropriate for families without this capacity. Honest assessment at enrollment is essential.

Addressed By

Monthly Community Days

Concentrated, high-quality peer time. Project exhibitions, collaborative work, shared meals, and movement — all the community elements, once per month with full intensity.

Addressed By

Community Mentor Network

Active placement of students with community mentors for project support and Field Phase internships. Families access the network; the school maintains relationships and quality standards.

Addressed By

Groundwork Family Training

Required 4-hour family training before enrollment. Covers facilitation skills, the movement guide, how to use AI tools, when not to help, and how to reach the advisor. Ongoing support throughout.

Choosing Your Path

Which Model Is Right
for Your Family?

The research doesn't dictate a single delivery format — it dictates the elements that must be present. Each model ensures those elements are present. The choice is about your family's situation, not about which is "better."

Choose In-Person School if...
  • →You want the full daily community experience for your child
  • →Both parents work full-time and need a full school day
  • →Your child thrives on daily peer interaction and structured environment
  • →You want the full movement infrastructure, campus environment, and specialist teachers
  • →You prefer to be the family partner rather than the home teacher
Choose Hybrid if...
  • →You want community and structure but also meaningful family time on weekdays
  • →One parent works part-time or from home and can facilitate home days
  • →You value the research on family involvement and want to be more actively involved in daily learning
  • →Your child does well with both community time and independent work
  • →You want a transition path — either toward more campus time or toward independent study
Choose Independent Study if...
  • →A committed adult is available to facilitate learning at home daily
  • →Your family travels, lives rurally, or has scheduling constraints that make campus attendance difficult
  • →You are deeply engaged in your child's education and want to be the primary teacher
  • →Your family has professional or entrepreneurial networks that could enrich the Field Phase
  • →You are willing to actively build peer community through co-ops, sports leagues, and monthly gatherings

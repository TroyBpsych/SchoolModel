02 — The Movement Program

Physical Activity
Is Academic Infrastructure

The research is unambiguous: movement isn't a break from learning — it is the mechanism that makes learning possible. The Groundwork Model treats physical activity as a core academic requirement, not a reward or recess.

🏃
Type 1 · Aerobic

Sustained Cardio Movement

Running, cycling, swimming, vigorous play, and team sports. The strongest evidence base for academic gains — specifically math and overall achievement. Triggers BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), the protein that supports neuron growth and synaptic plasticity.

He et al. 2025 · SMD = 0.22 overall achievement
  • Minimum 20–30 continuous minutes per session
  • Moderate intensity — elevates heart rate
  • Outdoors preferred over gym when possible
  • Daily — not 3x/week
🚲
Type 2 · Balance & Coordination

Cycling, Climbing & Balance

Bike riding, balance beams, climbing structures, gymnastics, and coordination drills. These activities specifically activate the prefrontal cortex — the region responsible for executive function, decision-making, and focus. Especially powerful for younger children during the critical developmental window.

League of Bicyclists 2025 · Prefrontal cortex activation
  • Balance bikes introduced in Roots Phase
  • Cycling infrastructure on campus (paths, bike storage)
  • Climbing walls or structures in outdoor space
  • Balance challenges integrated into warm-ups
🎭
Type 3 · Improvised Movement

Unstructured Creative Play

Child-designed games, free movement, improvisational physical activity. Research shows improvised movement significantly outperforms structured PE for developing divergent thinking — the same cognitive capacity at the heart of creativity and problem-solving. The mechanism: real-time physical decision-making activates creative neural networks.

ScienceDirect 2025 · Divergent thinking activation
  • Daily unstructured outdoor play — not directed by adults
  • Student-invented games encouraged
  • Large open outdoor space for free movement
  • Especially critical after cognitive work sessions
🤸
Type 4 · Micro-Movement

Movement Breaks & Desk Activity

Short movement breaks every 20–30 minutes of seated work, standing desks, wobble stools, and brain breaks. Prevents the cognitive performance drop that follows extended sitting. For grades 1–3 specifically, several shorter outdoor breaks distributed across the day outperform one longer block — the same total minutes, better sustained.

Hendershot 2022 · LiiNK Project distributed recess research
  • No more than 20 consecutive minutes of seated work for ages 5–10
  • No more than 30 consecutive minutes for ages 10–14
  • Grades 1–3: movement distributed across 3+ outdoor blocks, not 1
  • Standing desks and wobble stools available throughout
🌿
Type 5 · Outdoor Nature Time

Green Space & Nature Exposure

Time in natural environments — not just outdoors on pavement. Nature specifically restores directed attention through what researchers call "soft fascination" — effortless engagement that replenishes the focused attention depleted by academic work. Up to 2.3 hours per day positively associated with math, language, and reading.

BMC Public Health 2023 · 2.3h/day sweet spot
  • Campus includes green space, gardens, and natural surfaces
  • Outdoor learning is a regular instruction option
  • After-lunch outdoor time always in nature, not hallways
  • School garden maintained by students
⚽
Type 6 · Team Sports & Cooperative Play

Sports & Collaborative Games

Team sports build emotional intelligence, collaboration, resilience, and the capacity to handle both success and failure in a real-stakes environment. Regular sports participation improves concentration which then drives academic gains. Sports are part of the character curriculum, not separate from it.

Sember et al. 2020 · Concentration as academic mediator
  • Available daily, not only in PE class
  • Multiple sport options — not just mainstream team sports
  • Cooperative games alongside competitive ones
  • Student-organized leagues and challenges
Why It Works — The 5 Mechanisms
01

BDNF Production

Exercise stimulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor — the protein that grows new neurons and strengthens synaptic connections. Called "Miracle-Gro for the brain" by Harvard researchers.

02

Cerebral Blood Flow

Aerobic exercise increases oxygen and glucose delivery to the brain — directly fueling the metabolic demands of learning and memory consolidation.

03

Executive Function

Physical activity directly strengthens working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control — the three components of executive function that predict academic success.

04

Attention Restoration

Nature exposure and outdoor movement restore directed attention capacity that gets depleted by sustained academic work — allowing students to re-engage cognitively.

05

Emotional Regulation

Endorphin release from exercise reduces cortisol (stress hormone), lowers anxiety, and creates the emotional conditions necessary for learning and memory formation.

03 — Instruction Design

How, How Long &
In What Sequence

Research identifies not just what to teach, but how to teach it, how long each mode should last, and in what sequence. The Groundwork Model treats instructional design as a science, not a preference.

Layer 1 · 15–20 min max

Direct Instruction

Teacher-led, explicit, focused

What it is: Teacher introduces a new concept or skill with clear explanation, modeling, and worked examples. Checks for understanding throughout. No passive listening — students respond, predict, and demonstrate.

When to use it: Foundational skills (phonics, arithmetic procedures, grammar), new domain knowledge, and any time students lack the background knowledge to discover productively. Project Follow Through — the largest education study ever conducted — found explicit direct instruction was the only approach with consistently positive outcomes for all learner types. For K–3 reading specifically, the National Reading Panel's meta-analysis and subsequent IES research are unusually unambiguous: systematic, explicit phonics instruction is necessary, not optional — see File 15 for the full case.

Time limit: Research on attention and cognitive load shows that sustained passive listening degrades after 10–15 minutes. Direct instruction segments should not exceed 15–20 minutes without a response activity, movement break, or transition to practice.

Layer 2 · 20–30 min

Guided Practice

We do it together

What it is: Students practice the new skill with teacher support — working examples together, checking work in real time, catching misconceptions before they calcify. Immediate, specific feedback is one of the highest-leverage interventions in all of research (Hattie d=0.73).

When to use it: Immediately after direct instruction, before students work independently. Research shows that moving to independent practice too early — before adequate guided practice — produces frustration, errors, and discouragement. The sequence is non-negotiable: I do → We do → You do.

AI role here: Adaptive AI tools extend guided practice by giving each student individualized immediate feedback — effectively giving every student a personal tutor for foundational skill work.

Layer 3 · 90–120 min

Project Studio

Problem-based, inquiry, making

What it is: Students work on authentic, complex, multi-week projects. Teacher acts as coach, questioner, and connector — not instructor. This is where conceptual depth, creative thinking, collaboration, and real-world application happen. The longest block of the day, requiring sustained focus.

Why this long: Deep work requires extended time. Research on flow states (Csikszentmihalyi) shows that meaningful creative and intellectual engagement requires at least 45–60 minutes to reach depth. 90–120 minute blocks allow students to actually get into the work, hit obstacles, and push through — the cognitive sequence that produces real learning.

Movement built in: Within the Project Studio, 2-minute movement micro-breaks occur every 25–30 minutes. Students working on physical or outdoor projects naturally integrate movement.

Layer 4 · 20–25 min

Reflection & Metacognition

Daily portfolio practice

What it is: Students document what they learned, what they made, what confused them, and what they'd do differently. This is not journaling for its own sake — it is metacognition, the deliberate practice of thinking about one's own thinking.

Why it matters: Hattie's synthesis of 1,200+ meta-analyses rates metacognitive strategies at effect size d=0.69 — among the highest-leverage interventions known. Students who develop strong metacognitive habits outperform peers on virtually every academic measure, and the skill transfers across all domains and all of life.

Timing: End of day, when the full day's learning is available to reflect on. 20–25 minutes is sufficient; beyond this, diminishing returns.

Timing Science — What Research Says About Duration
Attention Research

The 20-Minute Rule

Sustained attention to passive instruction degrades significantly after 10–15 minutes. After 20 minutes without an active response, retention drops sharply. This is not about student willpower — it is neuroscience.

Rule: No passive instruction segment longer than 15–20 minutes. Always followed by a response, practice, or movement activity.
Deep Work Research

The 90-Minute Depth Window

Meaningful creative and problem-solving work requires extended uninterrupted time. Research on flow states and cognitive engagement shows the deepest work occurs between 45 and 120 minutes into a focused session. Short class periods prevent students from ever reaching this depth.

Rule: Project Studio blocks are minimum 90 minutes, uninterrupted except for 2-minute micro-movement breaks every 25–30 minutes.
Schedule Research

The 8:30am Biological Floor

Adolescent circadian biology shifts during puberty, making early rising biologically harmful — not a habit problem. The American Academy of Pediatrics mandates no academic instruction before 8:30am for middle and high school. The Seattle study showed a 34-minute sleep increase and 4.5% grade improvement with a 55-minute start delay.

Rule: No academic instruction before 8:30am for Workshop and Field phases. Day begins with advisory — relational, low-cognitive-load.

05 — The Full Day

Every Hour Earns
Its Place

Three phase-specific daily schedules — each built from the research up. Every block justified by evidence on timing, duration, instruction mode, and the physical and cognitive needs of students at that developmental stage.

Start Time
8:30am — not 8:00am
Young children rushed through breakfast and arriving dysregulated cannot learn. Research supports no earlier than 8:30am even for the youngest students.
Day Length
6 hours (ends 2:30pm)
Full-day beats half-day in the research, but 7 hours pushes the upper biological limit for ages 5–9. 6 hours with a regulation reset is the evidence-supported sweet spot.
Explicit ≠ Pressured
A research distinction
Short, systematic, multisensory direct instruction in phonics and math is evidence-based from Kindergarten onward — this is not the same as worksheet drilling or high-stakes pressure. The two are often wrongly conflated.
How Much Is Academic? — The Time Budget
Even at Age 9, Direct Instruction Is Under 10% of the Day

This is the question every parent asks: how much of the day is "real academics"? The research in File 15 gives a precise answer — explicit, systematic instruction in reading and math is necessary and evidence-based, but it occupies a small, concentrated portion of the day. Movement, guided play, project work, arts, and community make up the rest — and the research shows they aren't competing with academics. They're what make academics work.

The headline number: explicit, teacher-led direct instruction is roughly 15 minutes/day (4%) in TK/K, growing to 30 minutes/day (8%) by 3rd grade — still under 10% of the school day at age 9. Add guided academic practice (independent reading, structured math practice) and the combined "core academics" time is ~11% in TK/K and ~33% by 3rd grade. 06 — Three Phases

Not Twelve Grades —
Three Developmental Phases

Students advance when they're ready, not when the calendar moves. Each phase has a distinct learning environment, instruction model, movement program, and assessment approach.

Ages 5–10

The Roots Phase

What It Looks Like

Play-based learning, guided and free, with explicit instruction in early literacy and math woven in through short, multisensory, engaging blocks — never joyless drill, never decontextualized worksheets. Rich dramatic play spaces, outdoor time throughout the day, and instrument introduction from age 5.

Across the phase, the balance shifts: in TK/Kindergarten, guided play dominates and explicit instruction is brief (under 10% of the day). By 1st–3rd grade, systematic phonics and a concrete-to-abstract math sequence take a clearer daily shape — but even at age 9, explicit direct instruction stays under 15% of the day. The majority of every day, at every age in this phase, remains movement, play, project work, arts, and community.

07 — Family Partnership

Family Isn't a Guest —
Family Is the Co-Teacher

The APA review of 448 studies is unambiguous: the type of family involvement matters more than the amount. The Groundwork Model is designed to activate the kinds of engagement that research shows actually move outcomes — and stop asking families to do the things that don't.

What the Research Says Works — and What Doesn't
✓ What actually works
💬

At-Home Conversations About Learning

Talking about ideas, asking questions about what the child is working on, discussing the news, or debating a topic together. Especially powerful for older students.

Strong positive effect across all ages · APA 448-study review
📚

Reading With and To Children

Reading aloud together, taking children to the library, and surrounding them with books at home. Positive impact on reading, comprehension, language development, and attention.

Positive across all developmental stages · Harvard Family Research
🙌

Expressing Belief and Encouragement

Telling children you believe they can grow, that struggle is part of learning, and that their effort matters — consistently, specifically, and genuinely.

Consistent positive effect across all ages · Youth.gov synthesis
↔️

Two-Way Communication With the School

Regular meaningful dialogue between advisor and family — not just newsletters and report cards. Families who communicate regularly with teachers are more involved at home.

Strong positive effect on achievement AND engagement · National PTA research
🔢

Structured Math Support at Home

When schools give families specific, actionable guidance for supporting math — number games, counting activities, talking about patterns — proficiency improves significantly.

Higher math proficiency · Johns Hopkins research 2005
✗ What doesn't work (or backfires)
📝

Helping With Homework

The APA review found that parental homework help has a small negative impact on academic achievement — particularly in secondary school. It removes the productive struggle that builds competence and can create dependency and conflict.

Small negative academic effect · APA meta-review 2019
🚁

Hovering and Over-Managing

"Helicopter parenting" undermines the autonomy and self-regulation that Self-Determination Theory identifies as essential for intrinsic motivation and deep learning. Children need room to struggle.

Reduces autonomy and self-regulation · SDT research · Deci & Ryan
🎪

Attending Events Without At-Home Engagement

Showing up to school events without meaningful engagement at home produces only a weak effect on actual learning. School attendance is not a substitute for the daily relational environment at home.

Weak effect on academic outcomes · Youth.gov synthesis
📣

One-Way Communication From School

Newsletters, grade reports, and one-directional emails inform families but don't engage them. Research shows two-way communication — where families have voice and response — is the active ingredient.

Minimal impact without reciprocal dialogue · Harvard Family Research Project
The Six Standards of Family Partnership — How Groundwork Implements Each

Based on the National PTA's six research-backed standards for family engagement. These are the structural commitments the Groundwork Model makes to every family — not aspirations, but designed-in practices.

01

Two-Way Meaningful Communication

Families and advisors communicate regularly — not just when something is wrong. Communication flows both directions: school informs families, families inform the school.

Groundwork does this: Weekly advisor-family check-in (text, call, or note). Family Learning Guide sent home at the start of each 8-week project cycle. Families can message their advisor directly at any time. Exhibition nights create shared language around student growth.
02

Parenting Support

Schools help families understand child development — what's normal, what's a signal, and how to support growth at home without creating pressure or dependency.

Groundwork does this: Annual family orientation with developmental research summaries by phase. Monthly "Research in Plain English" newsletter explaining what studies show about supporting children. Advisor answers parenting questions as a trusted partner, not a gatekeeper.
03

Partners in Learning at Home

Families receive specific, actionable guidance for how to support learning at home — replacing homework with high-value family activities grounded in research.

Groundwork does this: Family Learning Guides for each project cycle include 3–5 conversation starters, a shared book recommendation, and one activity tied to the current project. No traditional homework. Family activities are optional, specific, and designed to be joyful.
04

Families Welcome at School

Families have real roles — not just as event attendees, but as genuine contributors to school life. Their expertise, experience, and knowledge are treated as assets.

Groundwork does this: Families invited to contribute to project cycles as subject-matter experts. Community Mentor Network draws heavily from parent community. Open-studio policy — families can observe project work any time. Shared lunch — parents can eat with students on designated days.
05

Decision-Making Partners

Families have real voice in school governance — not just feedback on decisions already made. Research shows that parent decision-making roles increase investment and trust.

Groundwork does this: Family Advisory Council with genuine input on policy, curriculum themes, and school direction. Annual family survey results shared publicly with school response. Phase transition decisions made collaboratively between advisor, student, and family — not unilaterally by school.
06

Community Resources for Families

The school connects families to resources — not just educational ones — that strengthen the whole family's capacity to support their child. Especially critical for families under economic or social stress.

Groundwork does this: Resource guide for local family support services maintained and updated. Advisor trained to identify and connect families to external support. School partnerships with local organizations. Explicit equity lens: all families can effectively support learning when given the right support.

The Dinner Table — The Most Underestimated Academic Tool

Harvard's Home-School Study of Language and Literacy Development produced one of the most surprising findings in family research: dinner table conversation is a more powerful vocabulary builder for young children than reading aloud. Dinner surfaces "rare words" — sophisticated vocabulary adults use naturally in stories — in a warm, contextual setting where children absorb language effortlessly. The CASA 10-year longitudinal study found teens who had 7 family dinners per week were 40% more likely to receive A's and B's. The benefits span language, academic achievement, mental health, and protection from risky behaviors — and they hold across every income level and culture studied.

Academic

Higher Grades

Teens with 7 family dinners/week are 40% more likely to receive A's and B's. Effect holds across 10 years of longitudinal data.

CASA 10-year study 2005
Language

Vocabulary Growth

Dinner conversation exposes children to more "rare words" than read-aloud. Preschool dinner talk predicts vocabulary and reading scores through 6th grade.

Harvard Home-School Study · Snow & Beals 2006
Mental Health

Wellbeing & Resilience

Regular family meals associated with higher self-esteem, reduced depression, reduced anxiety, and a stronger sense of family identity and belonging.

Harrison et al. 2015 · Armstrong-Carter & Telzer 2020

Risk Reduction

Teens with frequent family dinners significantly less likely to use substances. Protective effects hold 1 and 5 years later in longitudinal follow-up.

Project EAT · Eisenberg et al. 2004
✓ What makes dinner powerful
  • →No devices at the table — this is the single most important condition. Screens reduce conversational depth and eliminate most of the benefits.
  • →Adults tell stories about their day — not just ask about the child's. Hearing sophisticated adult language in narrative context is the vocabulary mechanism.
  • →Open questions that require thinking — "What surprised you?" "What's something you figured out?" rather than "How was school?"
  • →All family members speak — children need practice producing conversation, not just receiving it.
  • →Frequency matters — 5 or more dinners per week produces the strongest outcomes. Even 3–4 produces meaningful benefits.
How Groundwork Supports This
  • →Every Family Learning Guide includes a Dinner Table Edition — 3 conversation starters tied to the current project cycle that naturally spark the narrative, exploratory conversations the research shows are most beneficial
  • →No homework is assigned — so family dinner time is never competed with or rushed through to finish an assignment
  • →Family orientation includes the dinner research explicitly — parents understand why this matters and how to make it work
  • →Evening school events are scheduled with awareness of family meal times — the school never routinely disrupts the dinner hour
The Research in One Paragraph

The APA's review of 448 studies found that at-home discussion, encouragement, and reading with children are among the most powerful things a family can do — while helping with homework shows a small negative effect on achievement. The Harvard Family Research Project identifies two-way communication as the active ingredient in school-family partnership. The National PTA's six standards provide the structural framework. And one finding cuts across every study: all families can effectively support their children's learning when given the right guidance — the gap between highly and less-involved families is largely a gap in information and school support, not in motivation or love.
